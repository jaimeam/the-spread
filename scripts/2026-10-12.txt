Here's how it could reach markets, briefly.

Offshore crude comes first. Platform evacuations and shut-ins trim US supply for a short time. Gulf-produced medium sour grades are hit hardest. If the platforms are undamaged, restarts usually follow within days.

Refining is the bigger question. If the storm went into the Texas and Louisiana refining belt, plants may have cut runs or lost power. Flooding of process units is the real risk. The US Gulf Coast is Europe's swing diesel supplier, which means fewer export barrels would show up first in northwest European diesel cracks. Latin American importers of Gulf gasoline and diesel would lose barrels too. Buyers in the US Northeast compete with Europe for those same barrels, and that would support the New York Harbor diesel premium.

Port closures can halt exports even where refineries keep running. That would mean a short gap for European buyers, and then a wave of cargoes arriving together.

Two more channels to watch. Neither has a reported impact today. Most US liquefied natural gas, or LNG, export capacity sits on that same coast. Any terminal or shipping-channel disruption would delay cargoes, and that matters for prompt European gas prices at the Dutch TTF hub going into winter. Gulf Coast ethane crackers also often shut ahead of storms, and they are slow to restart.

The simple way to read what comes next is this. If it was a shut-in-and-restart storm, output and refinery runs get back to normal within about a week. The crude impact is small, with at most a brief bump in diesel cracks.
