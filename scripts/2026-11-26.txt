Houthi drones over Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery reversed an early slide in crude on Wednesday and lifted Brent above ninety-three dollars. Separately, a record share of large product tankers is now hauling crude, which keeps the cost of moving diesel and jet fuel high.

The Houthis say they struck Ras Tanura, the kingdom's largest refinery. Saudi Arabia's account is different. It says its air defences destroyed four drones over the site on Wednesday morning, and coalition spokesman Turki Al-Maliki said fragments fell from the interceptions, with no injuries in an initial assessment.

So the claim of a hit comes from the Houthis alone, and Riyadh describes interceptions only. Nothing in the reports we've seen today says whether any refinery units or loadings were affected.

Crude turned on the news. Brent had been at ninety dollars ten in early Asian trade, according to OilPrice dot com, before the gains came, and Rigzone noted they came in thin pre-holiday trading. If the Houthis can reach the Gulf coast, both Saudi export coasts are exposed, since they've already ordered ships out of Saudi Red Sea ports.

Staying with shipping, the product tanker market is being reshaped. A record seventy-five percent of the global fleet of Long Range Two tankers, the big clean-product carriers, is now hauling crude instead, according to OilPrice dot com. The number and share still in clean trade are at record lows.

It comes down to earnings. Crude pays better since the Middle East disruption, so owners are moving these ships into dirty trade, gCaptain reported. That leaves fewer large clean tankers and keeps fuel freight expensive. Long-haul buyers of diesel and jet carry that cost, and Europe most of all. As of mid-November, Kpler said the transatlantic arbitrage on medium range tankers was already shut for December arrivals.

An update on Hormuz. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps navy says two tankers hit mines early Wednesday off the southern and eastern coasts of the strait, causing engine-room fires. It says both were on routes Iran hasn't authorised. That's Iran's claim only, and no ships have been named.

On the other side of the water, the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, ADNOC, has restricted loaded crude and product tankers entering waters near some major UAE export hubs, Rigzone reported. Which hubs, and for how long, wasn't given.

Over in China, refiners are buying more crude abroad to rebuild inventories after the price fall, according to traders cited by Bloomberg. Sinopec alone has reportedly bought about twenty million barrels of West African crude this month, for December loading. For scale, all Chinese buyers together take twenty to twenty-five million barrels from West Africa in an average month.

Purchases from the Middle East and Brazil are up too. That extra demand lands on Atlantic Basin grades, the same barrels other Asian buyers are already paying premiums for.

Turning to gas, British households face a steep rise. Ofgem, the energy regulator, confirmed the price cap will go from about one thousand seven hundred and twenty pounds to about two thousand one hundred and seventy. The change takes effect on the first of January, a rise of twenty-six percent. It's the highest level in over three years, driven by wholesale gas prices lifted by the war.

Relief doesn't look close. Petronas expects the liquefied natural gas market to stay relatively tight through next year.
