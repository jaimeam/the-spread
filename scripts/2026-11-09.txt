This is The Spread, your energy markets briefing for Monday, the ninth of November. This episode is written and read with AI.

Iraq says Washington has agreed a lane for its oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz, near Oman. Only Baghdad has said so. Meanwhile, US forces disabled a ship trying to run the Iran blockade, after a week in which crude prices fell hard on bets that peace is coming faster than the fighting suggests.

On the markets. Brent ended last week around ninety-nine dollars a barrel, down about fifteen percent, according to OilPrice dot com. Rigzone reports that was the biggest weekly drop since 2022. December West Texas Intermediate lost eleven dollars forty on the week and touched eighty-six dollars seventy. EU gas storage was seventy-two point seven percent full on Saturday, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe, down from seventy-three point eight on Wednesday.

Iraq's foreign minister, Fuad Hussein, said on Sunday that Baghdad has reached understandings with the United States. Those would let Iraqi crude transit Hormuz through a lane near Oman, Reuters reported. There is no US statement in the reports, and neither the route nor any volume has been given. If it holds, Iraq's medium-sour barrels would move on a US-sanctioned route rather than through one-off transits.

The claim comes as Gulf supply recovers. A Bloomberg survey puts October OPEC output close to pre-war levels, led by Saudi Arabia. Iraq's oil minister says the country already pumps more than five million barrels a day. That is over half a million above its OPEC plus requirement, which is awkward six days after Baghdad agreed to hold December targets unchanged.

For some buyers, ships are now the binding constraint. The chairman of Hindustan Petroleum told Bloomberg the Indian refiner is struggling to hire tankers for Gulf loadings, even with crude flows near pre-war levels.

Staying in the region, the blockade of Iran turned kinetic again over the weekend. On Saturday a US military aircraft fired on and disabled a commercial vessel trying to break the blockade in the Gulf of Oman, according to US Central Command. That evening, explosions hit southern Iran. Iranian media framed them as Iran's own military activity rather than US strikes, and both sides reported ships hit.

Crews are paying the price. A seafarer was killed on Thursday in a suspected missile strike on the Velos Sapphire, a Greek-managed product tanker of about seventy-five thousand deadweight tonnes, inside the Gulf. Iran has also reshuffled its Hormuz blacklist, Splash twenty-four seven reported.

The consultancy FGE NexantECA says Iranian exports are near zero under the blockade. It sees slim odds of a deal and calls the next two months the period of highest escalation risk.

Back to last week's sell-off, where the trigger was political. Republicans lost both chambers of Congress, and Vice President Vance blamed the war, according to OilPrice dot com. That fed bets on de-escalation.

The banks' balances point the same way. JPMorgan estimates the market ran a surplus of one point one million barrels a day in September. It sees that widening to one point four million in October as high prices cut demand. Product markets moved first. Asia's high-sulphur fuel oil margin slipped to a discount of two dollars thirty a barrel on Thursday, as more Gulf oil reaches Asia.

Over in Russia, Ukraine has now hit a sixth Russian refinery within a week, Rigzone reported. Three were hit between the thirtieth of October and the second of November. The latest are new strikes, not a recount, though damage and idled capacity aren't reported.

Moscow's budget shows the strain. Subsidies paid to producers to keep road fuels at home are at their highest in more than four years. The diesel export ban has already been extended, so every lost run means less Russian product for buyers abroad.

Separately, Russia says overnight strikes hit three cargo ships carrying military goods for Ukraine, plus two substations. That is Moscow's claim alone, with no ship names and no response from Kyiv.

On shipping, Trafigura, Vitol, Mercuria and the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, or ADNOC, are negotiating with US officials. They want licences to buy or lease about fifty sanctioned tankers, and Treasury is weighing approvals, according to Bloomberg. The report rests on unnamed sources, and Treasury hasn't commented.

Freight is already reshaping crude pricing. Soaring tanker rates have pushed sellers to deepen discounts on heavy grades, Reuters reported, which gives US Gulf Coast refiners cheaper feedstock. According to the EIA, US imports of Venezuelan crude averaged seven hundred and one thousand barrels a day in July, the most since May 2017. They eased to six hundred and fifty-five thousand in August. More tonnage, if licensed, could narrow those discounts.

A few more things.

Energy Aspects now expects China to export about five hundred and eighty thousand tonnes of diesel in November, Bloomberg reported. That's four hundred thousand below its earlier estimate, because Chinese inventories have fallen, which leaves Asia's diesel market tighter. The figure could be revised up if domestic demand weakens.

Egypt has told fertiliser producers their gas supply will be cut by a further thirty-three percent, according to a company statement.

In Norway, the regulator Havtil is investigating a fire in the switchboard room on the Equinor-operated Visund field.

Adani Ports is buying the Dutch storage operator Evos for about two point six billion euros.

ADNOC has signed a ten-year liquefied natural gas deal with Gujarat Energy, starting in 2028. It's the company's fifth in India, and reported volumes differ between sources.

And the US rig count rose to six hundred and four, according to Baker Hughes. Oil rigs were up one, to four hundred and sixty-three.
