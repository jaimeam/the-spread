On the markets. At the Asian open, Brent was at ninety-one dollars sixty and West Texas Intermediate at eighty-five dollars eighty. The American Petroleum Institute estimates a nine million barrel crude build in the week to the sixth of November, reversing a draw the week before. Another two point seven million barrels left the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, and the Institute also reports a distillate build, without giving a figure. European gas storage stood at seventy-two percent full on the ninth of November, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe.

Starting in Russia. Two industry sources told Reuters that Ukrainian drone attacks since late October have cut the country's refining capacity by about thirty percent. Nine of Russia's thirty-five major refineries were disrupted over the past week. Together they account for about two point two million barrels a day of nameplate capacity.

The timing is awkward for Moscow. It is trying to raise diesel exports under an agreement with Washington, so fewer barrels are being made just as more are being promised abroad. That matters for anyone buying distillates, which remain the main prop for refining margins, according to a note from the analytics firm Kpler. Crude that can't be refined at home may end up exported instead, provided the ports are intact. The reports give no timelines for damage repair or restarts.

Meanwhile, the first Russian diesel cargo bound for the US has stopped off the American east coast, Reuters reported. Its operator says no destination has been decided. The US energy secretary says he's confident it will eventually be delivered.

Turning to crude prices. Front-month Brent touched eighty-nine dollars seventy early Tuesday in Europe, according to OilPrice dot com. That was its first move below ninety since August. Crude has now fallen almost thirty dollars a barrel since the midterms.

The trigger was President Trump. After meeting Qatar's prime minister, he said he's keen to pursue diplomacy and wind down the war, and signalled no strikes on Iran for now, Rigzone reported. There were also reports that Washington would let Iran enrich uranium to almost four percent. Official sources have not confirmed that.

The catch for refiners is that physical flows are recovering faster than costs are falling. Vessel tracking has shown Gulf exports close to pre-war levels for over a month, and Middle East benchmarks are weakening. But war cover, insurance and record tanker rates still keep delivered costs high, and rates for very large crude carriers from the Gulf to China have dipped only marginally.

China is buying again. Its October crude imports came to about nine point nine million barrels a day, according to Chinese customs data. That's the highest since March and up nine point four percent on September. It's still thirteen point three percent below October last year.

For American drivers, there's some relief. Regular gasoline averaged just under four dollars a gallon on Tuesday, according to the American Automobile Association. That's the first reading below four dollars since July.

Over in the Gulf, the attacks haven't stopped. A vessel belonging to ADNOC Logistics and Services was damaged in an attack in UAE territorial waters on Tuesday, according to gCaptain and World Oil. Separately, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre reported an apparent projectile strike on a tanker near the Strait of Hormuz.

In Iraqi Kurdistan, production at the Khor Mor gas facility is still suspended after a suspected drone attack and fire on the eighth of November. The operators, Dana Gas and Crescent Petroleum, say damage is still being assessed.

The Houthis say they hit pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, along with Abqaiq and Riyadh's King Khalid airport. That is a claim only, and Riyadh has not acknowledged any attacks. The pipeline feeds Yanbu, the Saudi export outlet that bypasses Hormuz.

Shipowners and insurers are looking for ways to keep tankers moving. The Saudi shipping company Bahri says it is sending tankers through Hormuz in coordination with the US military. And President Macron is discussing a publicly backed vehicle with Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states to take on part of the war-risk insurance for Hormuz transits, Rigzone reported. The Gulf states have not commented.

Now to gas. Qatar's LNG loadings have picked up over recent weeks to around half of pre-war levels, according to Reuters. Ras Laffan remains under force majeure, which leaves buyers in Europe and Asia still short of what they had before the war.

Thirty-one LNG tankers crossed Hormuz in October, up from twenty-seven in September, according to Kpler. That's the most since February, and most of those cargoes loaded at Ras Laffan. Two were moved onto other tankers after clearing the strait.

In the US, a warm start to November is easing the picture. The EIA now expects almost seventy billion cubic feet more gas in storage at the end of the month than it forecast in October.
