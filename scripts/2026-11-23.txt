This is The Spread, your energy markets briefing for Monday, the twenty-third of November. This episode is written and read with AI.

Oil posted another weekly loss even as tankers kept getting hit near Oman. Supply is plentiful, US sour crude has lost its war premium, and Gulf barrels are flowing into China. Meanwhile the Houthis have told ships to leave Saudi Red Sea ports, which puts Yanbu, the Hormuz bypass, in their sights.

On the markets. Brent fell about five percent last week to around ninety-one dollars, according to OilPrice dot com, and Rigzone notes that was its third straight weekly loss. January West Texas Intermediate lost more than five percent. Early Friday, Brent was at ninety-two dollars twenty, and West Texas Intermediate at eighty-two dollars seventy. Baker Hughes counted six more US oil rigs, taking the total to four hundred and sixty-nine.

The biggest shift is in sour crude. The price of US sour grades has fallen hard, Bloomberg and Rigzone reported, as barrels from Venezuela, Canada and the Middle East surge and refiners find they have plenty to choose from. Neither report puts a size on the move.

China shows the pull most clearly. Imports from Gulf producers other than Iran reached nineteen point seven million tonnes in October, according to a South China Morning Post calculation from Chinese customs data. That was up forty-two point four percent from September, and the highest since the war began. Gulf producers cut prices to win buyers while freight sat at record highs.

For sour-heavy refiners on the US Gulf Coast and in Asia, that's a reversal. The scarcity that squeezed their margins for most of the year has turned into a choice of cheap barrels. And as medium-sour supply returns, the premium for light-sweet crude could widen again.

Out in the Gulf, the attacks haven't stopped. On Friday a tanker was struck by a projectile in the Strait of Hormuz and caught fire, gCaptain reported. On Saturday, according to Reuters, an outbound tanker was hit on the stern by an unidentified projectile about eleven nautical miles north of Khasab, in Oman. A separate vessel reportedly struck a mine east of the strait.

None of these reports names the attacker. Bloomberg's tally shows attacks peaked in October at the highest level of the war and have continued almost daily this month. Crude volumes through Hormuz have dipped in recent days, though the reports don't say whether the attacks are the cause.

Indian refiners are feeling it on the freight side, Rigzone reported. They're rushing to lock in tankers on long-term charters.

Turning to the Red Sea. The Saudi-led coalition says it struck the Yemeni port of Hodeidah on Friday to destroy Houthi capabilities, according to gCaptain. The Houthis responded by ordering ships to leave Saudi Red Sea ports, warning airlines away from Saudi airspace and promising new strikes.

Earlier on Friday, media reported Houthi strikes on Riyadh, hitting the financial district, the sovereign wealth fund's headquarters and a petrochemicals company. Those reports come from media only, with no Saudi confirmation. Brent briefly rose to ninety-two dollars seventy on the news.

What makes this matter is Yanbu. It's the Red Sea outlet Saudi Arabia uses to get around Hormuz, so a credible threat there lands on the very route that has carried diverted barrels. So far, no effect on loadings has been reported.

On Iran, the blockade is being tested. Last week Washington widened its naval blockade of Iranian ports to cover ship-to-ship transfers. Since then, a cargo of Iranian crude has moved inside the Gulf from a sanctioned Suezmax to an unsanctioned medium-range tanker, according to United Against Nuclear Iran and the tracking firm Kpler. The first of those is an anti-Iran advocacy group.

OilPrice dot com reads the transfer as Iran preparing to restart exports. Tehran hasn't confirmed that. Washington, for its part, is leaning on the transit hubs. Treasury officials met regulators in Singapore and Malaysia to warn them about sanctions compliance, gCaptain reported. Both countries handle transfers of Iranian crude headed for China.

The Treasury's sanctions office also issued new Iran designations on Friday, though the notice names no one. Separately, Iran again refused UN inspectors access to its nuclear sites.

Saudi Arabia and Japan announced a storage deal on Friday. Japan will buy up to eight million barrels of Saudi crude to rebuild the national reserve it drew down during the Hormuz crisis, if market conditions are favourable. In return, Saudi Arabia gets temporary use of a further sixteen million barrels of Japanese storage, on top of what it already holds there. Bloomberg and Rigzone report it has begun refilling those tanks.

Over to European gas. Storage stood at sixty-nine point eight percent full on Wednesday and sixty-nine point two percent on Saturday, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe. Net withdrawals already equal about eleven percent of the gas injected this summer. That summer total was fifty-one point two billion cubic metres.

Norway is partly offsetting the draw. It sold eleven billion cubic metres in October, according to the Norwegian Offshore Directorate. That's up about two billion from September, or roughly three hundred and fifty-six million cubic metres a day.

A few more things.

Vladimir Putin ruled out negotiating after Ukraine's strikes on the Crimean Bridge, and Russian missiles hit Kyiv's infrastructure on Friday. In the Gulf of Finland, a Russian military-linked cargo ship burned after a suspected drone strike.

India's demand for liquefied petroleum gas reached two point six million tonnes in October, an eight-month high, according to government data.

The US national average for gasoline was three dollars seventy a gallon on Friday, according to AAA.

And in Australia, Inpex approved the next offshore phase at its Ichthys liquefied natural gas plant. The work is meant to hold output at plateau, which is nine point three million tonnes a year.

What to watch next. Daily EU gas storage readings run through the third of December. On Wednesday the EIA publishes its weekly report for the week to the twentieth of November. Distillate stocks stood at about one hundred and five million barrels a week earlier. The following report, for the week to the twenty-seventh, comes on the second of December.

Further out, there's Guyana's first Uaru cargo and Pakistan's first tender cargo in mid-December, the EU ban on long-term Russian liquefied natural gas from January, and the end of Russia's diesel export ban that same month.
