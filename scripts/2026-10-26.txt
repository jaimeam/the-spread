Brent closed last week above one hundred and sixteen dollars after further attacks on Saudi Arabia. That came even though Middle East crude exports are now running above pre-war levels. Freight is the other squeeze: tanker costs are pricing US crude out of Asia and pushing fuel oil above crude in Singapore.

On the markets. Brent topped one hundred and sixteen dollars a barrel on Friday, according to Rigzone, and OilPrice dot com had it above one hundred and seventeen by Friday evening. Goldman Sachs puts the global average crude freight rate above twenty dollars a barrel, against three dollars a year ago. EU gas storage stood at seventy-four point six percent full on the twenty-fourth of October, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe.

Starting in the Gulf. Friday's rally built through the day, with Brent touching one hundred and fourteen dollars intraday that morning before pushing higher as the attacks on Saudi Arabia escalated, Rigzone reported. What makes it unusual is the supply picture. According to Kpler, October Middle East oil exports ran higher than before the US and Israeli strikes on Iran at the end of February.

The physical market isn't following futures. Prices for some Middle East crudes sold outside the Gulf actually fell on Friday. So the premium in Brent reflects the risk of losing Saudi barrels, not a shortage today. Refiners buying on futures-linked formulas pay that premium anyway, even with cargoes available.

What's still unclear is the damage. The reports we've seen today don't say which Saudi sites were hit, or whether output was affected. A statement from Aramco or the Saudi energy ministry would settle it.

Also in the region, Ras Al Khaimah closed all its beaches on Saturday after an offshore oil leak the day before, Reuters reported. The source of the leak hasn't been given.

Now to shipping, where freight is cutting US crude off from Asia. Earlier this month a voyage from the US Gulf Coast to China cost as much as eighty million dollars, according to OilPrice dot com. That's about ten times the pre-war norm. It has since eased, but only to around sixty million.

At those rates there's little reason to send US crude east. gCaptain reports that US crude exports to Asia are on course for multi-year lows, possibly the weakest in seven years. More light sweet crude stays in the Atlantic Basin as a result, and Asian refiners lean on supply closer to home.

The cost is feeding into products too. Singapore high-sulphur fuel oil now trades above the crude it's made from, OilPrice dot com reported. Behind that are lost Middle East and Russian supply, steady bunker demand and the cost of shipping replacement cargoes. Very-low-sulphur fuel oil may ease towards year-end, if more Gulf supply and extra Chinese export quotas arrive.

Turning to diesel. The Russian cargo bound for the US has sailed, and is expected in Norfolk, Virginia, early next month, according to gCaptain. A second cargo is heading for New York. It would be the first Russian diesel to reach the US in more than three years, though volumes haven't been reported.

Europe's diesel and jet supply remains tight. The European Commission says there's no physical shortage, according to Rigzone. Germany says it will try to stop further refinery closures, but hasn't detailed any measures.

On gas, that storage figure was down a hundredth of a point on the day. More to the point, it's the same level as on the eighteenth of October. Net injections have been flat for about a week, just as Europe heads into winter.

Britain is expected to lean more heavily on imports this winter, Rigzone reported. Its proven and probable reserves fell to two point five billion barrels of oil equivalent at the end of last year, according to the North Sea Transition Authority. That's a drop of zero point four billion. No exploration wells were drilled in that year at all.

Back in the US, the Department of Energy on Friday authorised an emergency exchange from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Up to four hundred thousand barrels will move from the West Hackberry site to the Phillips sixty-six refinery at Lake Charles. The department cited a temporary disruption to crude deliveries and gave no cause. The reserve is already at its lowest since nineteen eighty-two, OilPrice dot com noted, and this release keeps one Gulf Coast plant running rather than adding product supply.

And in the Black Sea, a drone strike sank a cargo ship off Romania on Friday, Reuters reported. One person was killed. Three are missing. Romania's president blames Russia. Separately, Russia's defence ministry says its drones hit a cargo vessel bound for Chornomorsk and a second ship overnight into Sunday, which Kyiv hasn't confirmed in today's reports.

A few more things. Hurricane Isaias shut-in figures from the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement are still unpublished, overdue since the sixteenth of October. And the Office of the US Trade Representative has proposed keeping the suspension of port fees on China-linked ships in place into January next year.
