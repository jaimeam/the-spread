Two tankers were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, and this time the attacks are confirmed by maritime security warnings, not just Iranian claims. Even so, oil fell, as forecasts of a supply glut outweighed the risk in the Gulf.

Starting in the Gulf. United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations issued separate warnings about two tankers attacked in Hormuz on Monday. Both had engine-room fires, and one was left disabled. Recent hits had rested on Iran's word alone, so this is the first maritime-security confirmation of attacks on a single day.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards tell their own story. They say two tankers struck mines on Monday after taking a southern route Iran has not authorised, and that US officers guided them with fighter-jet support. That account is Iran's alone. The reports don't name an attacker, and it isn't clear whether these are the same two ships. Statements from the owners or flag states would settle it.

Iran also struck Bahrain for the second time in three days, Rigzone reported, while saying a path to ending the conflict remains open. The market barely flinched, and one account carried by gCaptain says the war hasn't stopped crude reaching buyers. For owners of supertankers, the risk premium is unwinding fast, with rates down more than sixty percent from October's record.

Down the coast, the Red Sea route is under new pressure. Yemeni military sources say Houthi fighters are within a hundred kilometres of Aden after government forces pulled back from positions near the Bab el-Mandeb. The Houthis have not given their own account.

The strait matters because Saudi crude loaded at Yanbu passes through it on the way to Asia. Yanbu is the outlet that bypasses Hormuz. Any threat there reaches the very barrels that have been replacing Gulf loadings, and that is the backdrop to Murban gaining while Brent fell. Container lines, meanwhile, are heading the other way: Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd are returning three more Gemini services to Suez, according to gCaptain.

Turning to the supply picture. The EIA now expects a global oil glut this quarter. Goldman Sachs goes further, seeing supply exceeding demand by two million barrels a day in twenty twenty-seven. It expects Brent to average eighty-eight dollars next year, then slide toward seventy-eight by year-end, citing weaker Chinese demand for transport fuel.

New barrels keep arriving. ExxonMobil has started Uaru, its fifth project on the Stabroek block, World Oil reported. That lifts Guyana's installed capacity to one point one million barrels a day.

Gulf crude is getting cheaper to buy. Kpler reports deeper discounts on Mideast Gulf grades as Asian refiners turn to Atlantic barrels. Chinese independent refiners are chasing Iraqi cargoes through trading houses, Bloomberg reported, because the US blockade has halted Iranian supply and Russian crude has become dearer. Iraq is also trucking crude out through Syria.

Refined products haven't followed crude all the way down. US diesel has dropped below six dollars a gallon, according to OilPrice dot com. Jet fuel stays high, though, and that lands on airlines just as their price hedges expire.

Now to gas. Pipeline flows to US liquefied natural gas plants hit a record twenty-one point eight billion cubic feet on Saturday, according to BloombergNEF. That's up twelve percent on a year ago. Golden Pass set a record, and Sabine Pass reached its highest since April. Add milder weather forecasts, and that explains Monday's slide in European prices.

Storage is still the constraint. EU stocks slipped about two tenths of a point on Sunday. According to APA-Economics, net withdrawals so far equal about a tenth of the fifty-one point two billion cubic metres injected this summer.
