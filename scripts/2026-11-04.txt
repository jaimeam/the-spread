Starting with the Gulf. The November Dubai contract has moved into contango for the first time since early July, according to OilPrice dot com. That's a sign Middle East crude supply now exceeds demand.

The reason is volume. Gulf producers have stepped up shuttle shipping through Hormuz, moving cargoes ship to ship in the Gulf of Oman once tankers clear the strait. Physical flows have been rising gradually for two months.

This matters for Asian refiners. The Dubai cash-to-futures spread is routinely used to set official selling prices for Gulf grades, so a flatter curve feeds straight into what Asian buyers pay. That could mean softer official prices for medium sour crude loading in December.

Cheaper freight is also reopening long-haul trades. Bharat Petroleum bought one million barrels of West Texas Intermediate for delivery to Mumbai, Reuters reported. The cargo arrives between late December and early January, priced at about eighteen dollars a barrel over Dated Brent. Freight has eased from the records it hit in early October.

The crude surplus hasn't reached refined products. Asian crude imports are back at pre-war levels, according to OilPrice dot com. But imports of gasoline, and especially diesel, from the Middle East have collapsed, because refining capacity there is reduced. OilPrice expects the diesel crunch to run well into next year.

Globally, there's been a partial recovery. Kpler data show refinery inputs fell by four point eight million barrels a day in the second quarter. That was a six percent drop. In the third quarter, throughput recovered by one point eight million barrels a day as refiners adapted to new trade flows.
