On the markets. January Brent traded at ninety-five dollars ninety at noon Eastern time on Thursday, up one point three percent on the day. That's up from a close of eighty-nine dollars forty on Monday. West Texas Intermediate was at eighty-four dollars seventy, after touching seventy-nine dollars ten earlier in the week, all according to OilPrice dot com. In Europe, gas storage was sixty-seven point two percent full as of Wednesday, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe.

Turning to the Red Sea. The Houthis hit Jeddah and Jazan, and the exchange of fire with Saudi Arabia shows no sign of easing, Rigzone reported. This comes after Wednesday's attack on Ras Tanura on the Gulf coast. The Houthis now say that strike caused fires, while Saudi Arabia had said the drones were intercepted, according to OilPrice dot com.

No damage has been reported at either coast so far. A statement from Saudi Arabia or Aramco on loadings would show whether exports were touched. Prices moved anyway, and Standard Chartered argues the market now needs a fresh shock to fall further. That's the bank's view, not a consensus.

The Red Sea coast matters because it's Saudi Arabia's bypass for barrels that would otherwise go through Hormuz. With its ports under attack, that outlet is now exposed alongside the Gulf. Charterers lifting from the Red Sea could face higher war-risk premiums as a result.

Over in the Gulf itself, tanker traffic through Hormuz has picked up even as the violence has grown. According to Vortexa data reported by Bloomberg, the seven-day average of crude, fuel and liquefied natural gas tankers was above twenty-six a day for most of this month. That ran from the first to the nineteenth of November. Earlier in the war it was below twenty-five on at least two-thirds of days. All this came while attacks and mine incidents were at their highest of the war, according to gCaptain.

Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy says a liquefied natural gas carrier hit a mine and exploded. It claims the ship had switched off its tracking and used a route Tehran considers illegal. The ship hasn't been named, and the claim is unconfirmed.

There's more detail on the restriction from the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company. A Petroleum Ports Authority notice bars tankers carrying crude, products, liquefied gases and chemicals from moving near the main export facilities, Splash twenty-four seven reported.

Meanwhile, commentators close to the Guard Corps are pushing for a declared tanker convoy to sail through the US blockade. They pair that with threats against Gulf neighbours' oil infrastructure if US forces fire on Iranian ships, according to OilPrice dot com. The same reporting projects Iran's crude and condensate revenue reaching zero by mid-December. This is advocacy, not a government decision. Kpler's note from the nineteenth of November saw signs Iran may test the blockade with small tankers, but no breakout in exports.

On corporate deals, a US-led group has signed for Lukoil's foreign business. A consortium led by Eldridge, which includes the US International Development Finance Corporation, has agreed to buy Lukoil International, according to World Oil. Until Thursday this rested on press reports. Now the buyers have announced it themselves, Rigzone reported, though there's no word from Lukoil.

The portfolio covers about three hundred and fifty thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day of production. That's spread across eleven countries, plus three European refineries. It's a share swap, and it still needs approval from the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control. If it closes, those refineries move from sanctioned Russian ownership into US-allied hands.

Turning to gas, Europe's storage draw is quickening in the cold. The daily withdrawal on Wednesday was point six three of a percentage point, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe. On Monday it was point five zero.

Dutch network operator Gasunie wants the Netherlands to build a strategic gas reserve, though it says there's no immediate shortage risk, OilPrice dot com reported. In Britain, the National Energy System Operator flagged a margin shortfall of more than two point seven gigawatts for Thursday afternoon and evening. That was the third tight day in a row for the UK power system.

Some relief may come from Egypt. A liquefied natural gas carrier off Idku points to a possible restart of exports after a nine-month pause, according to Kpler.

A few more things.

Two delegates say OPEC plus is weighing a rollover of current quotas into January, citing uncertainty from the Iran war, Rigzone reported. Iraq and Kazakhstan both want higher capacity assessments, and a rollover would push those fights past Sunday's meeting. Nothing has been decided.

Equinor has cut its growth guidance for this year to about three percent, from four. Bacalhau was shut in after a dropped object, Visund is offline after a fire, and Johan Castberg is at minimum rates because of offloading problems, according to World Oil.

In Libya, two Libyan National Army officers wounded in the Benghazi strike on the twenty-second of November have died, and the army has vowed retaliation. No restart at the Sharara field has been reported.
