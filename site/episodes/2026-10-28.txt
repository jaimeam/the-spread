Saudi Arabia has contained a fire near Ras Tanura, its biggest export terminal, after a Houthi missile and drone attack, and Brent firmed in early Asian trade. At the same time, Aramco is offering discounted spot crude to Europe from outlets that avoid the Strait of Hormuz.

On the markets. Brent traded at one hundred and fifteen dollars seventy early this morning, up one point six percent, according to OilPrice dot com. West Texas Intermediate was at one hundred and one dollars twenty, up one point three percent. The American Petroleum Institute estimates US crude stocks fell by about two million barrels in the week to the twenty-third of October. European gas storage stood at seventy-four point nine percent full on the twenty-sixth, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe.

Saudi Arabia said early Tuesday it intercepted three Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at the Eastern Region. It said it also intercepted four drones aimed at the south. A fire then broke out near the Ras Tanura export hub and was quickly put out, Bloomberg reported, citing an unnamed source.

The Houthis say a ballistic missile hit the Ras Tanura refinery, according to gCaptain. Riyadh hasn't confirmed that, and no damage to the terminal or the refinery has been reported. The site is on the Gulf side. Any outage would land on loadings that already depend on Hormuz transits and ship-to-ship transfers.

This morning's gain follows a drop of nearly two dollars on Tuesday. Rigzone put that drop down to signs of de-escalation diplomacy in the Gulf. Pulling the other way are reports that Washington is preparing a new campaign against Iran after the midterms. Neither the diplomacy nor the campaign is a confirmed plan; both rest on reports.

Over to Russia, where crude is giving way to diesel. Russian crude exports from Baltic and Black Sea ports could fall by one hundred to two hundred thousand barrels a day in November, compared with October. That comes from two traders working from preliminary loading schedules, Reuters reported, and the figures include Kazakh crude piped through Russia.

The reason is that refiners are running harder for diesel under the deal with Washington struck on the ninth of October. That leaves Western buyers of Urals with fewer barrels, while more Russian diesel comes up for export. Russia's seaborne crude shipments are already at their lowest in more than a month, according to gCaptain, even though the key Baltic port has recovered from its halt.

Analysts still expect the diesel shortage to last into next year, OilPrice dot com reports, and they say the US-Russia deal won't change that. Separately, Rigzone reports that new Indian refining capacity will lift diesel exports next year.

Back to Saudi Arabia, and a change of tone from Aramco. It is offering European refiners extra crude for October and November loading, Bloomberg reported, citing a person familiar with the matter. Late-October spot cargoes are priced below the official selling price.

The cargoes load at Yanbu on the Red Sea, or at Sidi Kerir on Egypt's Mediterranean coast. Only weeks ago, Aramco told customers they'd get no oil under October contracts after the East-West pipeline was attacked. Shipping from these outlets keeps the barrels clear of Hormuz. European refiners get medium-sour crude at a discount, and other Atlantic Basin sour grades may find themselves competing harder for European runs. Aramco is also close to a ten billion dollar bank loan, according to Rigzone.

In the Strait of Hormuz itself, Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority says it will take legal action against tankers owned by Sinokor, gCaptain reported. Sinokor is the world's biggest tanker owner. It has carried millions of barrels out of the strait in recent weeks.

For now this is Iran's statement alone. No action against the ships has been reported. Even so, the backdrop is busier. More than thirty incidents have been recorded in and around Hormuz this month, according to OilPrice dot com, against fourteen in September. The latest target was a crude tanker in Iraq's exclusive economic zone.

For tanker owners, the view on Wall Street is getting richer. Evercore ISI has doubled its earnings forecast for very large crude carriers next year, from fifty-seven and a half thousand dollars, Splash twenty-four seven reported.

A few more things.

OPEC plus is expected to leave December quotas unchanged, sources told Reuters. Its one point six five million barrel a day rollback finished in September, but eight months of war have made targets largely notional, so physical flows matter more than the decision.

India's crude basket reached one hundred and twenty-three dollars thirty a barrel last week, according to local media. Along with a weak rupee, that has left fuel marketers losing money even after raising pump prices. Oil Minister Hardeep Puri denied US pressure to cut Russian imports and said purchases follow price, as Russian arrivals decline and Middle East imports rise. Fuel export earnings reached twenty-seven point two billion dollars, according to the oil ministry. That covers April to September, and it is up fifty percent in rupee terms.

On deals, Dangote's refinery share sale hit its one point six billion dollar domestic target, sources told Bloomberg. TotalEnergies completed an eight hundred million dollar sale of its Nigerian Renaissance joint venture interest, World Oil reported. Eni is leaving Nigeria's onshore upstream, according to Rigzone. And Woodside is selling its Greater Sunrise stake for up to two hundred million dollars.

US offshore regulators' shut-in figures for Hurricane Isaias are still pending. They were due on the sixteenth of October.
