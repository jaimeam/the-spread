President Trump says the US no longer needs a deal with Iran, and Brent briefly jumped back to ninety-four dollars a barrel on the news. In the US, stocks built across crude, gasoline and diesel, and with the diesel export route to Europe shut for December, those barrels are staying at home.

On the markets. Earlier in Asian trade, Brent had been at ninety-one dollars thirty, with West Texas Intermediate at eighty-five dollars thirty, according to OilPrice dot com. Crude is still about twenty percent below its peak of one hundred and eighteen dollars, set in late October. US commercial crude stocks rose nearly four point seven million barrels in the week to the sixth of November, according to the EIA. EU gas storage stood at seventy-one point two percent full on the eleventh of November, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe.

Starting with Iran. On Thursday, Trump posted that the US has no need for a deal with Tehran, a change in tone from Monday, when he suggested the war was nearing its end. He also claimed Washington controls Iran's money and the Strait of Hormuz. That is his assertion, and Tehran has not responded in the reports we've seen today.

Brent had spent the morning drifting lower on peace hopes for both Iran and Ukraine, according to OilPrice dot com. The Ukraine side gave the market little to work with. Talks in Abu Dhabi on the tenth and eleventh of November agreed in principle on a prisoner exchange, but made no progress towards a ceasefire.

Meanwhile, Gulf shipping is still taking hits. A projectile struck the engine room of the Liberia-flagged crude tanker Dominica Prosperity off the United Arab Emirates on Wednesday, and all twenty-two crew were evacuated, gCaptain reported. Oman is also managing a tanker that ran aground off Al Qibliyah Island, including what happens to its cargo.

For shipowners, the price swings are showing up in fuel bills. Bunker prices have fallen by up to twenty percent in under two weeks, according to Splash twenty-four seven.

Turning to US products. Distillate stocks reversed in the week to the sixth of November, rising four point one million barrels to about one hundred and three million, according to the EIA. The week before, they had fallen by about three point six million. Gasoline rose one point eight million barrels. Total commercial petroleum stocks were up eleven point four million, with refineries running at ninety-five point two percent of capacity.

The catch for Gulf Coast refiners is that Europe isn't pulling those barrels. Weaker cash differentials in Northwest Europe have shut the transatlantic arbitrage for every December arrival on Medium Range tankers, according to Kpler. Late-December margins on that route fell about five dollars thirty a tonne. That leaves them at minus three dollars thirty.

Kpler reckons a four percent drop in forward freight would reopen the route. Until then, the Gulf Coast's swing diesel stays in the US, and it's adding to domestic stocks. Separately, the EIA raised its Brent forecasts for this year and next in its latest Short-Term Energy Outlook, though the report we've seen doesn't give the new figures.

Over in Asia, Gulf crude has overtaken Russian barrels in India for the first time since the war began. So far in November, India has taken about two point one million barrels a day from the Gulf, according to Kpler. Russia supplied about one point eight million. The shift reflects recovering flows through Hormuz, and Russian crude losing its price advantage.

Gulf grades are getting cheaper as they fight for buyers. The Dubai spread between the first and third months has collapsed from about eleven dollars a barrel to about one dollar, according to Kpler, and Gulf producers have cut their official selling prices. Prompt Agbami cargoes into Ningbo are about four dollars a barrel more competitive than Murban.

Other Asian refiners are shopping further afield. Traders and shipbrokers, who weren't named, say Sinopec and SK Energy have bought African and Brazilian grades as long-haul freight eases, Reuters reported. Russian sellers may now face pressure to deepen discounts if they want Indian demand back.

Also from Reuters, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations has agreed a joint crude stockpile. The storage will sit in Indonesia and Thailand.

Now to European gas. Storage slipped about a third of a percentage point on the day, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe. Germany was at fifty-eight percent in the previous update. Asian spot liquefied natural gas is trading above thirty dollars per million British thermal units, according to OilPrice dot com.

Even so, Rystad's winter outlook doesn't call a further rally. It lays out as many paths down as up.

Europe is still leaning on Russian liquefied natural gas ahead of the ban in January. The EU paid an estimated eight point seven billion euros for Yamal cargoes from January to October, according to Urgewald and Kpler. That is already more than it paid in all of last year.

Finally, supply news from the Atlantic basin. ExxonMobil has taken a final investment decision worth twelve and a half billion dollars on Longtail, according to World Oil. It is the eighth development on the Stabroek block, and Exxon's first gas-condensate project offshore Guyana.

Longtail is designed to process one point two billion cubic feet of gas a day. It is due to produce up to two hundred and fifty thousand barrels a day of condensate, starting in twenty thirty.

In West Africa, Nigeria pumped one point five million barrels a day of crude in October, according to its upstream regulator. That keeps it at its OPEC quota for a sixth straight month.
