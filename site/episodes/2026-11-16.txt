This is The Spread, your energy markets briefing for Monday, the sixteenth of November. This episode is written and read with AI.

The International Energy Agency says the oil market has tipped into surplus for the first time since the Iran war began, as Gulf exports recover. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is still badly constrained, though, and Iran claims two more vessels hit mines there over the weekend.

On the markets. Brent settled near ninety-six dollars a barrel on Friday, up from about ninety-one dollars thirty early Thursday. December West Texas Intermediate still lost two dollars sixty on the week, or two point nine percent. That made it crude's second straight weekly fall.

Starting with that surplus call. The agency's monthly report on Friday said global supply now exceeds demand, and it expects a surplus in the fourth quarter. Middle East exports are recovering faster than demand, despite continued attacks on shipping. The report doesn't say how big the surplus is.

Iraq did much of the lifting. Its October crude output reached four point six million barrels a day, the highest since twenty nineteen. That's up about five hundred and forty thousand barrels a day from September. Kuwait and Saudi Arabia also raised output.

Those barrels are getting out on smaller tankers that leave Hormuz and then transfer cargoes to very large crude carriers in the Gulf of Oman. That keeps exports moving even while big-ship transits stay thin.

Buyers in Asia are taking them. Bloomberg reported that Chinese, Japanese and Thai refiners are buying more Gulf crude, including cargoes that must cross the strait. Freight has come off record highs and producers are offering discounts. That account rests on unnamed traders. If it holds, Asian refiners may draw less on Atlantic Basin crude.

Prices didn't follow the bearish balance late in the week. Brent's Friday rebound came on attacks on Saudi infrastructure and continued threats to shipping.

Staying in the strait. Iran says two ships hit mines there on consecutive days, and neither strike has been independently confirmed. On Friday, the navy of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said a liquefied natural gas carrier struck a mine and suffered an explosion while using what it called an unauthorised route.

On Saturday, Iran's navy said an oil tanker caught fire after hitting a mine in the eastern strait. That came via Iranian state media, and Reuters could not verify it. The reports we've seen today give no vessel names or owners, and no British maritime security reports.

Even with exports recovering, shipping remains the bottleneck. Vessel traffic through Hormuz is about sixty-five percent below pre-conflict levels, according to gCaptain. Airlines in Asia are feeling it most directly. Industry executives told Reuters that Asian jet fuel prices are about seventy-five percent above pre-war levels. Carriers whose hedges are expiring now have to decide whether to lock in at those prices.

Turning to Russia. Ukraine has now struck nine Russian refineries since the start of November, according to Rigzone, up from six by the ninth. Damage and lost refinery runs haven't been reported.

The pressure goes beyond refineries. On Sunday, drones struck three commercial ships near Romania's Sulina canal, according to Romania's prime minister, and fighter jets were scrambled. Britain, meanwhile, sanctioned eleven ships tied to Russian energy exports, including five gas carriers and five oil tankers. It also listed nine companies, among them Slavneft and Rosneft's Vostok Oil project.

None of that has dented Moscow's income yet. The International Energy Agency says Russia's October revenue from crude and product exports was the highest since May, lifted by war-driven prices.

In Tehran, the outlook runs the other way. A senior budget official told the government to draft next year's budget on the assumption that oil revenue could fall to zero, OilPrice dot com reported. According to the US Treasury, Iran has loaded no crude since the twenty-fifth of August.

Its oil at sea has dried up too. Cargoes held outside the US blockade fell from nearly eighty-nine million barrels in mid-July to zero by mid-October. Chinese buyers pay one to two months after delivery, which is why the analytics firm Kpler expects the revenue to stop soon.

Separately, a restricted report from the International Atomic Energy Agency says Iran is still refusing inspectors access to verify its enriched uranium stockpile.

Over to gas. European power prices topped six hundred euros a megawatt hour last week during a wind drought, according to OilPrice dot com. That pushes more demand onto gas-fired generation just as storage is drawing.

Asia is short as well. Pakistan LNG Limited has reissued a tender for two cargoes into Port Qasim, after rejecting earlier bids, Business Recorder reported. One is for delivery in mid-December, the other in early January.

Back in the US, Washington extended the Jones Act waiver by ninety days on Friday. It's the third extension of a waiver first issued in March. A White House official confirmed it to Reuters, and a Customs and Border Protection notice set it out.

Foreign-flagged tankers can keep moving oil between US ports into February, though each voyage still needs individual approval under a restriction added in August. For Gulf Coast shippers, that means more tankers available to carry barrels to other US coasts through the winter.

A few more things.

ExxonMobil signed a memorandum of understanding with Venezuela's state oil company, PDVSA. It gives Exxon a year to evaluate Venezuelan oil areas, including its former Orinoco project, according to World Oil.

Baker Hughes says the total US rig count fell to six hundred and one, while oil rigs held at four hundred and sixty-three.

And Hapag-Lloyd has resumed some Red Sea services.

What to watch next. Daily EU gas storage readings from Gas Infrastructure Europe continue through the twenty-sixth of November. On Wednesday, the EIA publishes its weekly report for the week to the thirteenth.
